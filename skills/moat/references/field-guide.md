# Notes on "Moats in the Age of AI: The Field Guide for SaaS Founders"

The Field Guide is a short MicroConf PDF by Rob Walling and Einar Vollset, the companion to their MicroConf Europe 2026 talks and Startups for the Rest of Us episode 855. Download it at https://microconf.com/moats. These notes summarize how to apply it in an audit; they are not a copy of the guide. Use the original worksheet when scoring with a team.

## The short version

- AI can hit a SaaS business in ten ways. "My customers aren't technical" answers only one of them.
- The old moats (lots of code, a big feature set, integrations, switching costs, UX familiarity) are drying up.
- Eight moats still hold. Each is a spectrum, and none is perfect.
- Sell the outcome, not the tool: labor budgets are far bigger than software budgets.
- The most durable moat is a learning loop built on real work for real customers.
- Their estimate: 10 to 20% of SaaS companies will decline, plateau or go to zero because of AI.

## Attacks: the question to ask for each

Circle the 2 or 3 most likely in the next 12 to 24 months. Paraphrased check questions:

| # | Attack | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Your customer builds it | How few of our features does a typical customer use? |
| 2 | A competitor reaches parity | Could a newcomer match the core product in about 3 months? |
| 3 | Open source gets good enough | Is there an active open-source project in the category? |
| 4 | A platform ships your feature | Could our biggest integration partner build the core feature in a sprint? |
| 5 | Custom software at SaaS-ish prices | Could an agency build our bigger customers a custom version for a few hundred a month? |
| 6 | The model absorbs your product | If the next model release did our core job, what is left? |
| 7 | Your customer stops existing | Is AI shrinking our customers' whole industry? |
| 8 | Your seats go away | Is revenue tied to headcount that AI is shrinking? |
| 9 | Your user's job goes away | Could the main user be replaced by an agent within 2 years? |
| 10 | The step you manage stops mattering | Does the product manage a step that exists only because humans needed to coordinate? |

Attacks 8 and 9 can become opportunities: if AI is replacing the users, build that AI and sell it.

## The 8 moats: gut-check questions

Rob's file has what each moat means and his examples. The guide pairs each with one question:

| # | Moat | Gut check (from the guide) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Buyers know and trust you | "Who has built their business or career around recommending me?" |
| 2 | You're the system of record | "If a customer left tomorrow, what would break downstream?" |
| 3 | You're allowed to act | "Does a human still do the last step?" |
| 4 | They don't check your work | "What would customers never trust a homemade version to do?" |
| 5 | Someone official already said yes | "What approvals would a competitor need before a customer could switch?" |
| 6 | Everyone's already in it | "How many people would have to move together for one customer to leave?" |
| 7 | Every customer makes you smarter | "What do I know from real usage that a competitor couldn't simulate, scrape or buy?" |
| 8 | It's hard to keep running | "If a customer vibe coded me perfectly, what would go wrong in 6 months?" |

Several moats, or one very strong one, makes a business defensible. Moat 7 matters most because it is the one the authors expect to stay ahead of improving models. Their line: "A UX can be copied from screenshots. Knowledge learned from real-world results can't."

## Going on offense

The guide's three moves, summarized:

1. **Build faster.** Table stakes. Enough by itself only when the moats are already strong.
2. **Welcome the agents.** Offer an MCP server or API, control access, charge for it, and test whether an agent picks you when asked to solve your problem without being told your name.
3. **Become the worker.** The big one, which the guide says fits most founders. If a business pays someone to click your tool to get an outcome, someone will sell that outcome directly. Outcomes open labor budgets and build moat 7 along the way.

The 10x question: at $X/month today, what would the same customer happily pay $10X/month for?

If pricing is per seat: model what happens when the top 10 customers halve the relevant team. If that number is frightening, look at pricing on work done instead of people.

### Price-band advice (paraphrased)

- **Self-serve, under $100/month:** most exposed to attacks 1, 2 and 6. Invest in brand and trust now, keep the self-serve product as the entry point, and offer the worker as a premium, more sales-assisted tier.
- **Sales-led, mid-market:** strengthen champions and the system-of-record position, watch for agencies pitching custom builds to the biggest accounts, and pilot one outcome-based workflow with a few trusted customers.
- **Planning to sell in 6 to 18 months:** buyers want moats backed by evidence, an honest view of exposure, an agentic roadmap, and pricing that holds up.

Pick the band from the plan config: if most paying customers are on tiers under $100/month, use the self-serve advice even when a top tier costs more.

## Scorecard method

The guide's worksheet asks you to score each of the 8 moats from 0 (none) to 10 (dominant), write down evidence rather than hopes, circle the likeliest attacks, and then read off one of three bands:

- **Strong moats, low exposure:** build faster and get ready for agents.
- **One or two moats, some exposure:** strengthen the moat you're closest to, on purpose.
- **Weak moats, high exposure:** take becoming the worker seriously, and start now.

### Working rule for the bands (this skill's, not the guide's)

The guide's bands are qualitative. To apply them consistently, this skill counts a moat as "held" at 7 or above and "partial" at 4 to 6. Three or more held moats with at most one severe circled attack reads as strong. One or two held moats, or several partials, with some exposure reads as middle. No held moat, or circled attacks that hit the core job, reads as weak. Use judgment where the evidence disagrees with the count, explain the call, and label the rule as the skill's in the report.

### Scoring calibration (this skill's)

- 0: nothing in the product or business supports it.
- 1 to 3: a thin version exists, or it exists on paper but not in practice (a partner program with zero payouts).
- 4 to 6: real and present, with a named gap a competitor could route around.
- 7 to 8: strong, with evidence a newcomer would struggle to reproduce within a year.
- 9 to 10: dominant and category-defining (the accountants who recommend QuickBooks, Stripe Radar). Rare for a small SaaS; be suspicious of giving one.
